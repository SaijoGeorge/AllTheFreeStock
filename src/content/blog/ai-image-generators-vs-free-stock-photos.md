## The short answer

Use free stock photos when the image has to show something real or when you need a clear licence. Use an AI image generator when the picture you need doesn't exist yet, such as a concept, a stylised illustration or a background in exact brand colours.

Most pages ranking for this question are published by companies that sell AI generators, and they conclude that you should generate everything. Few mention who owns the result, what audiences think of AI imagery, or the disclosure rules now taking effect. This guide covers those, with a decision table, a scenario table and a hybrid workflow you can use today.

One framing question settles most cases: **does this image claim that something happened, or that someone exists?** A team photo, a customer story, a product shot and a news image all make that claim, and they need real photography. A blog header about cash flow, a pattern behind a headline, or a fantasy scene for a campaign don't, so a generator is fair game.

## How do AI images and stock photos compare?

Stock wins on ownership clarity, realism and trust. AI wins on specificity and speed to an exact concept. Cost is close to a draw at the free tier.

| Factor | Free stock photos | AI image generators |
| - | - | - |
| Legal clarity | Licence from a named contributor | Tool terms, often no copyright in output |
| Exclusivity | Non-exclusive, others use the same photo | Not guaranteed unique either |
| Cost (free tier) | Free, no download cap on major sites | Free, but rate-limited daily or monthly |
| Speed | Fast if the image exists | Fast per image, slow to get it right |
| Specificity | Limited to what was shot | Very high: any scene, colour, style |
| Realism | Real by definition (on AI-free sites) | Convincing, with errors in hands, text |
| People and likeness | Real models, usually with releases | No releases, risk of resembling someone |
| Brand consistency | Hard to match across photographers | Easier with reference images and styles |
| Editorial and news use | Suitable when captioned accurately | Unsuitable for depicting real events |
| Audience trust | Higher, real imagery expected | Lower, many people want labels |
| Disclosure | None needed | Increasingly expected or required |
| SEO and AI search | No penalty, originality still helps | No penalty, must keep AI metadata on product images |
## Who owns the image?

With stock, you get a licence from someone who owns the copyright. With a purely prompted AI image, there may be no copyright for anyone to own, which means you can use it but often can't stop others copying it.

### AI images and copyright

The US Copyright Office's January 2025 report on copyrightability concluded that AI outputs can be protected only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements, and that "the mere provision of prompts" isn't enough ([US Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html)). Human selection, arrangement and modification of an output can still be protected. On 2 March 2026 the US Supreme Court declined to hear *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, leaving the human authorship requirement in place ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/the-final-word-supreme-court-refuses-to-hear-case-on-ai-authorship)).

The tools' own terms point the same way. OpenAI's [Terms of Use](https://openai.com/policies/terms-of-use/) assign you its rights in output but warn that output "may not be unique across users". In practice, a competitor can generate something very close to your hero image, and you may have nothing to enforce. We go deeper on this in [Are AI images copyrighted?](https://allthefreestock.com/blog/are-ai-images-copyrighted/)

In Australia, the law on AI output hasn't been settled by a court yet. On training data, the federal government ruled out a text and data mining exception for AI training on 26 October 2025, and set up a reference group to consider licensing and whether to clarify how copyright applies to AI-generated material ([Simpsons Solicitors](https://www.simpsons.com.au/news/update-australian-government-rules-out-text-and-data-mining-exception-for-ai-training)).

### Training data risk

The other legal question is how the model was trained. In the UK, Getty Images' case against Stability AI ended in November 2025 with the High Court dismissing Getty's remaining secondary copyright claims and upholding only "extremely limited" trademark findings ([Cleary Gottlieb](https://www.clearygottlieb.com/news-and-insights/publication-listing/uk-high-court-issues-landmark-ruling-in-getty-images-v-stability-ai)). That's good news for generator users, though cases in other countries continue. If you want the lowest-risk option, Adobe says its Firefly models are trained on licensed content such as Adobe Stock and on public domain material, not scraped from the open web ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/ai/overview/firefly/gen-ai-approach.html)).

### Stock licences

Free stock licences are short and readable, and they come with specific limits. The [Pexels licence](https://www.pexels.com/license/) bans selling unaltered copies, showing identifiable people in a bad light, and implying that people or brands in the image endorse your product. The [Unsplash licence](https://unsplash.com/license) bars compiling its images into a competing service. Our guide to [free stock photo licences](https://allthefreestock.com/blog/free-stock-photo-licences-explained/) compares the major ones.

> **Rule of thumb:** If you need to stop other people using the image, neither free stock nor a raw AI output gives you that. Commission it, or add enough human design work that the final piece is clearly yours.

## Realism, people and likeness

Stock photos of people are real people who usually signed a model release. AI people have no release, and they can drift towards resembling someone real.

Generators are now good at photorealism, and they still slip on hands, small text, jewellery and how objects are held. Those errors matter most where people look closely: product pages, team pages and anything printed large.

People are the riskier case for a second reason. A generated face that happens to resemble a celebrity or a private person can create a likeness or misleading endorsement problem. The UK Advertising Standards Authority said in June 2026 that AI depictions of real people must not mislead consumers into thinking they endorse a product, and that its rules apply "regardless of how ads are created" ([Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/06/18/asa-warns-on-ai-generated-content-and-deepfakes-102n4o1)).

> **Common mistake:** Using an AI-generated "customer" next to a real testimonial. Even if the quote is genuine, the picture implies a real person said it, which turns an honest review into a misleading one.

## What do audiences think of AI imagery?

Audiences are more sceptical of AI images than marketers assume, especially when the images show people or products, and most want AI images labelled.

The strongest data points we found:

| Study | Sample | Finding |
| - | - | - |
| Getty Images VisualGPS, 2024 | 30,000+ adults, 25 countries | Almost 90% want to know if an image was made with AI, 98% say authentic images are central to trust |
| IAB and Sonata Insights, Jan 2026 | 505 US consumers aged 16 to 43, 104 ad executives | 82% of executives think these consumers feel positive about AI ads, 45% do |
| NielsenIQ, Dec 2024 | 2,000+ viewers, about 150 with EEG | AI ads called "annoying", "boring" and "confusing", weaker memory activation even when high quality |
| Washington State University, 2024 | 1,000+ US adults | Mentioning "artificial intelligence" lowered emotional trust and purchase intention |

| University of Melbourne and KPMG, 2025 | Australian sample of a global study | 36% of Australians willing to trust AI, 78% concerned about negative outcomes such as misinformation |

A few details sharpen the picture:

- **The gap is widening.** In the IAB study, the share of Gen Z consumers feeling negative about AI ads rose from 21% in 2024 to 39%, and 30% of Gen Z respondents described brands using AI ads as inauthentic, against 13% of Millennials.
- **Subject matters.** Getty's [full report](https://reports.gettyimages.com/VisualGPS-Building-Trust-In-AI.pdf) says sentiment skews more negative towards brands using AI to create people or products, and is more positive for buildings, structures and landscapes.
- **Disclosure isn't fatal.** In the IAB study, 73% of consumers said knowing an ad was AI-created would make them more likely to buy or make no difference, and clear disclosure ranked third among the things that drew their attention to an ad.
- **Vendor interest.** Getty and Storyblocks both sell human-made stock, so they have a stake in these results. The IAB, NielsenIQ and university studies don't, and they point the same way.

## Do you have to disclose AI images?

Sometimes, and the rules are tightening. The EU now requires machine-readable marking of AI output, Google and Meta read AI metadata, and Australia has voluntary guidance backed by consumer law.

| Rule or platform | What it requires | Applies to |
| - | - | - |
| EU AI Act, Article 50 | Providers mark output as AI in machine-readable form, deployers disclose deepfakes | Enforceable from 2 Aug 2026 |
| Google Merchant Center | AI images must carry IPTC `TrainedAlgorithmicMedia`, don't strip it | Product listings |
| Meta | Labels images with C2PA or IPTC AI signals, disclosure tool for photorealistic video and audio | Facebook, Instagram, Threads |
| Australian guidance | Voluntary labelling, watermarking and metadata | Businesses using AI content |
| Australian Consumer Law | Images and descriptions must not mislead | All advertising |
| UK ASA | Existing code applies to AI ads, no fake endorsements | UK ads |

Sources: [Baker Botts](https://www.bakerbotts.com/thought-leadership/publications/2026/september/eu-ai-act-article-50-transparency-obligations-go-live), [Google Merchant Center](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/14743464?hl=en-GB), [Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/02/labeling-ai-generated-images-on-facebook-instagram-and-threads/), [Department of Industry, Science and Resources](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/being-clear-about-ai-generated-content), [ACCC](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/advertising-and-promotions/false-or-misleading-claims).

The EU's provider obligations have a grace period to 2 December 2026 for generative systems already on the market. For Australian businesses, the federal guidance published on 28 November 2025 recommends three mechanisms: visible labels, watermarks and metadata, noting labels are more credible when backed by the other two. The ACCC's rule is that claims must be "true, accurate and based on reasonable grounds", and it counts "images and descriptions of what is offered" among the things that must be accurate. An AI image that makes a meal look bigger or a room look newer can breach that, labelled or not.

> **Quick check:** Before publishing an AI image, ask whether a customer would feel misled if they found out. If yes, change the image. If you're unsure, label it.

## Do AI images affect SEO and AI search?

Google doesn't penalise images for being AI-generated, but it does read AI metadata, and original images serve both readers and search better than generic ones.

Google's [guidance on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) focuses on whether content helps users. It warns that generating many pages "without adding value for users" may breach its scaled content abuse policy, and recommends adding image metadata so readers know how content was made. Google Images supports the IPTC Digital Source Type field, including `trainedAlgorithmicMedia` for generated images and `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia` for inpainted or outpainted photos ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/image-license-metadata)). Its "About this image" panel can also show C2PA data revealing whether AI tools made or edited an image ([Google](https://blog.google/technology/ai/google-gen-ai-content-transparency-c2pa/)).

Three practical points follow:

1. **Don't strip metadata.** Many image optimisers remove IPTC and C2PA fields to save bytes. For AI product images in Merchant Center, that breaks a requirement.
2. **Pick representative preview images.** Google's [image guidelines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/google-images) advise using an image that's "relevant and representative of the page" and avoiding generic ones.
3. **Originality earns attention.** Nielsen Norman Group's eyetracking research found readers study photos of real people, such as a company's own staff, and ignore decorative stock filler ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)).

> **Worth knowing:** AI answer engines pick sources they can trust, and a page with original, properly attributed images reads as more credible than one padded with generic visuals. ## Use stock when, use AI when

Stock is the default for anything that represents reality. AI is the default for anything that represents an idea.

| Use free stock when | Use an AI generator when |
| - | - |
| The image shows real people, places or events | The image illustrates an abstract idea |
| You need a licence you can show a client | You need a style or scene no library has |
| The context is news, health, finance or legal | The context is playful, fictional or conceptual |
| People need model releases | No identifiable people appear |
| The audience is sceptical of AI | You need exact brand colours or a set format |
| You'll use the image in print or large formats | You need many quick variations for testing |
| The client brief says "no AI" | You'll label it and keep its metadata |

## Which to use for common jobs

The framework applied to the images small teams make every week:

| Job | Best choice | Why |
| - | - | - |
| Blog header | Stock, or AI for abstract topics | Real scenes for practical posts, AI for concepts like "data privacy" |
| Product mockup | Stock mockup template, or your own photo | The product must look exactly as it is |
| Team or about page | Your own photos | Must be real, AI or stock people mislead |
| Testimonial or case study | Real customer photo | Implies a real person, never AI |
| Paid ad creative | Stock or own photo, AI for concept tests | Ads face misleading-claims rules and likeness risk |
| Social post | Either, AI for memes and illustrations | Label AI if it looks photographic |
| Editorial or news | Stock or agency photo, accurately captioned | Generated images can't show real events |
| Abstract background or texture | AI | No people, no claims, exact colours |
| Presentation slide | Either | Low stakes, check the licence or tool terms |
| Email header | Either | Keep it simple and on-brand |
| Product listing (ecommerce) | Your own photo | Merchant Center needs AI tags, buyers expect accuracy |
| Seasonal or campaign hero | Hybrid | Real base photo, AI-extended to fit the layout |

## A hybrid workflow: stock first, AI second, labelled

Start with a real photo, use AI only to adapt it, then keep and declare what you changed. This gets you specificity with far less trust and legal risk than a fully generated image.

1. **Find a real base image.** Search the libraries in [our directory](https://allthefreestock.com/) that ban AI uploads, such as Unsplash and Pexels, so your starting point is genuine.
2. **Check the licence for edits.** Most free licences allow modification. Watch for endorsement and likeness limits if people appear.
3. **Make targeted AI edits.** Extend the canvas to fit a banner, remove a distracting object, swap a background colour or match a brand palette. Avoid altering faces or adding people.
4. **Keep human design on top.** Add your own typography, layout and graphics. That improves the result and strengthens any copyright claim in the final design.
5. **Tag the file.** Set the IPTC Digital Source Type to `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia` and keep any Content Credentials your editing tool adds.
6. **Label where it matters.** A short caption or alt text note such as "Photo by [photographer], extended with AI" covers both credit and disclosure.
7. **Record the trail.** Save the source URL, licence, tool used and date in your asset notes.

> **Rule of thumb:** Let AI change the frame around a real moment, never the moment itself. Extending a sky is fine. Adding a crowd to an empty shop isn't.

## A 60-second checklist before you publish

Run these questions on any image, stock or generated:

- Does the image imply a real person, place, product or event? If yes, it must be real.
- Do you have a licence or tool terms that allow this use?
- Could a viewer mistake an AI person for a real customer or staff member?
- Does the image show the product or result accurately?
- Is the AI status in the metadata, and is it intact after optimisation?
- Does the brief, client contract or platform require disclosure?
- Would you be comfortable if a customer asked, "Is this real?"

If an image passes all seven, it's ready to publish.
